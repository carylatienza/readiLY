# **Diagnostics and Dysfunctions: A Comprehensive Analysis of Teacher and Student Pain Points in the Administration of Phil-IRI and CRLA (Grades 1–3)**

## **Introduction: The Landscape of Early Literacy Assessment in the Philippines**

The Philippine basic education system is currently navigating an unprecedented landscape of post-pandemic learning recovery, underpinned by a critical national mandate to eradicate systemic learning poverty. Literacy, universally recognized as the foundational cornerstone of all subsequent academic and cognitive achievement, has become the primary focal point of national educational policy. Recent international benchmarking assessments have illuminated the depth of this crisis. The 2019 Southeast Asia Primary Learning Metric (SEA-PLM) revealed that a staggering number of Filipino early-grade and intermediate learners exhibited the least proficiency in core learning areas, with the Philippines ranking as the second-least performer in reading, achieving only a 10 percent proficiency rate, trailing slightly ahead of Laos. Furthermore, systemic analyses of public elementary school pupil achievement indicate that reading performance typically lags up to three grade levels behind age-appropriate expectations.  
In response to these systemic deficiencies, and accelerated by the unprecedented 32-week educational hiatus caused by the COVID-19 pandemic in 2020, the Department of Education (DepEd) deployed a series of diagnostic literacy tools designed to rapidly map learning gaps and prescribe highly targeted interventions. For early grade learners in Key Stage 1 (Grades 1 to 3), the educational infrastructure relies heavily on two primary assessment mechanisms: the Comprehensive Rapid Literacy Assessment (CRLA) and the Philippine Informal Reading Inventory (Phil-IRI).  
The CRLA was introduced in collaboration with the ABC+ Project (Advancing Basic Education in the Philippines) as a rapid, five-minute diagnostic tool. It functions as an agile mechanism to gauge foundational Mother Tongue, Filipino, and English reading proficiencies, allowing teachers to immediately categorize learners into actionable instructional tiers. Conversely, the Phil-IRI is a much older, highly granular diagnostic inventory anchored on the "Every Child A Reader Program" (ECARP). It is utilized to measure oral reading fluency, silent reading, and listening comprehension through a rigorous miscue analysis matrix.  
While both tools are theoretically robust and empirically validated, their ground-level execution within Philippine public school classrooms presents a highly complex web of operational, pedagogical, and psychological challenges. The administration of these assessments—particularly the critical and overlapping convergence of both tools in Grade 3—has generated significant systemic friction. This report provides an exhaustive, nuanced examination of the pain points experienced by educators and early-grade learners during the implementation of the CRLA and Phil-IRI, exploring the resulting assessment fatigue, resource dependencies, technological hurdles, linguistic barriers, and the paradoxical realities of mandated literacy interventions such as "Catch-Up Fridays."

## **Diagnostic Architectures: Methodological Frameworks of CRLA and Phil-IRI**

To fully comprehend the operational challenges faced by early-grade teachers, it is necessary to deconstruct the architectural mechanisms of the CRLA and the Phil-IRI. While both assessments aim to diagnose literacy, their methodologies, scale, computational demands, and intended target audiences differ significantly, creating disparate administrative burdens.

### **The Comprehensive Rapid Literacy Assessment (CRLA)**

The CRLA was explicitly conceptualized as a crisis-response mechanism. Prior to the CRLA, there were no standardized tools or systems used to assess early reading in the Mother Tongue (MT) across the Philippines. The initial beginning-of-school-year (BoSY) tasks focused entirely on foundational elements: letter sounds, isolated words, and sentence reading. The tool's primary operational advantage was its brevity, designed to take roughly five minutes per child, enabling teachers to quickly triage returning learners after the prolonged pandemic lockdowns.  
The CRLA evaluates learners across varying languages depending on their grade level: Grade 1 learners are assessed in their Mother Tongue; Grade 2 learners are assessed in both Mother Tongue and Filipino; and Grade 3 learners undergo assessments in Mother Tongue, Filipino, and English. Based on their performance, the CRLA algorithmically categorizes learners into distinct reading profiles that dictate the required level of intervention.

| CRLA Reading Profile | Diagnostic Indicator | Intervention Required |
| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| **Full Refresher (Low Emerging)** | Learner scores 0 to 16 upon administration of Parts 1 and 2; cannot decode basic letter sounds or isolated words. | Comprehensive, highly intensive foundational remediation (phonemic awareness, alphabet knowledge). |
| **Moderate Refresher (High Emerging)** | Learner scores 17 to 30 on Part 1; reads less than 25% of the text and cannot answer any comprehension questions. | Targeted support focusing on decoding and basic fluency. |
| **Light Refresher (Developing/Transitioning)** | Learner scores 17 to 30 on Part 1; reads between 26-75% of text and answers 1 to 3 questions correctly. | Minimal feedback and guided reading practice to build fluency. |
| **Grade Ready (At Grade Level)** | Learner scores 17 to 30 on Part 1; reads above 75% of text and answers all comprehension questions correctly. | Ready for standard grade-level curriculum; requires advanced enrichment. |

### **The Philippine Informal Reading Inventory (Phil-IRI)**

In stark contrast to the rapid triage model of the CRLA, the Phil-IRI is a time-intensive, classroom-based assessment tool designed for deep pedagogical analysis. Revised extensively in 2018 under DepEd Order No. 14, s. 2018, the Phil-IRI package consists of a Manual of Administration, Group Screening Tests (GST) in Filipino (Grades 3–6) and English (Grades 4–6), Graded Passages, and complex recording forms.  
The assessment is administered in two distinct phases. Phase one is the Group Screening Test (GST), a silently administered 20-item comprehension test based on a leveled passage. Learners who achieve a raw score of 14 or higher (75%) are deemed to be reading at or above their grade level and are exempted from further testing. However, learners scoring below 14 must undergo phase two: the individualized Phil-IRI assessment using Graded Passages.  
During this individualized phase, the administrative burden on the teacher exponentially increases. The teacher must listen to the child read aloud and concurrently execute a Reading Miscue Inventory (Phil-IRI Forms 3A and 3B). This involves strictly timing the reading to compute the reading rate (words per minute), marking every single miscue (omissions, substitutions, insertions, mispronunciations), and asking a series of literal, inferential, and critical comprehension questions. The teacher must then utilize specific mathematical formulas to calculate the Oral Reading Score and the Comprehension Score to map the student onto the Phil-IRI Reading Profile matrix.  
The required calculations add severe cognitive load to the teacher during the assessment:

1. **Oral Reading Accuracy:** (Total Words in Passage \- Number of Miscues) / Total Words x 100\.  
2. **Reading Rate:** (Number of Words Read x 60\) / Reading Time in Seconds.  
3. **Comprehension Percentage:** (Number of Correct Answers / Total Number of Questions) x 100\.

By cross-referencing the Oral Reading Score and the Comprehension Score, the teacher determines if the learner is at the Independent (97-100% reading, 80-100% comprehension), Instructional (90-96% reading, 59-79% comprehension), or Frustration level (89% and below reading, 58% and below comprehension).

## **The Grade 3 Assessment Convergence: The Epicenter of Assessment Overload**

The most acute administrative bottleneck in Key Stage 1 occurs in Grade 3\. Based on official DepEd guidelines, Grade 3 learners occupy a unique transitional space where early literacy tools and intermediate literacy tools violently collide.  
A standard Grade 3 teacher is mandated to administer the CRLA in the learner's Mother Tongue, in Filipino, and in English to track the multilingual transition. Simultaneously, because Grade 3 is the entry point for the Phil-IRI Filipino assessments, the teacher must also administer the 20-item Group Screening Test (GST) to the entire class. Following the GST, the teacher must then conduct the lengthy, individualized Phil-IRI oral reading and miscue analysis for every single child who scored below 14\. In some school districts, teachers are additionally tasked with administering the Early Grade Reading Assessment (EGRA) and the Rapid Math Assessment (RMA) within this exact same beginning-of-school-year window.  
This massive convergence forces Grade 3 teachers to balance the rapid, triage-style testing of the CRLA across three separate languages with the granular, time-intensive miscue analysis of the Phil-IRI. The resulting operational chaos induces profound assessment fatigue for both the educator and the eight-year-old learner, frequently compromising the validity of the data collected.

## **Administrative and Logistical Asphyxiation: Teacher Pain Points**

The theoretical benefits of data-driven reading interventions are consistently undermined by the systemic realities of the Philippine public school classroom. Educators tasked with executing the CRLA and Phil-IRI face an interconnected web of administrative, logistical, and competency-based challenges that drastically limit their instructional efficacy.

### **Assessment Fatigue and the Compliance Paradox**

The modern DepEd teacher operates in an environment characterized by chronic, systemic assessment overload. The sheer volume of diagnostic mandates—CRLA, Phil-IRI, EGRA, RMA, and the Multi-Factored Assessment Tool (MFAT) for learners with special needs—creates a "compliance paradox." Tools that are theoretically designed to free up instructional capacity by identifying exact learning gaps instead consume vast amounts of instructional time.  
Teachers report being heavily bombarded with non-teaching workloads, coordinatorship roles, and repetitive paperwork, which drastically reduces the time available for actual classroom instruction and reading remediation. The Department of Education has formally acknowledged this phenomenon at the highest policy levels. DepEd Order No. 15, s. 2026 (Revised Guidelines on Classroom Assessment, Grading Systems and Awards), explicitly cited "assessment overload" and "assessment fatigue" as persistent challenges that weaken the developmental purpose of educational evaluation. The Order seeks to limit written works and performance tasks to ease this burden, noting that when the assessment process becomes overly procedural, teachers enter a state of compliance-driven exhaustion. Instead of utilizing diagnostic data to inform pedagogy, fatigued educators merely encode numbers to satisfy regional submission deadlines, thereby defeating the purpose of the inventory.

### **The "Rapid" Misnomer and Severe Time Management Constraints**

Although the CRLA was conceived as a five-minute rapid assessment, the reality of the public school classroom severely skews this timeline. Conducting individualized, one-on-one assessments is highly disruptive when a teacher is simultaneously responsible for managing a class of 40 to 50 early-grade learners. While a "Grade Ready" learner might breeze through the CRLA tasks in three minutes, a "Full Refresher" who struggles with basic phonemic awareness can take up to ten minutes per language assessment, as the teacher must patiently wait for responses, offer encouragement, and manage emotional meltdowns.  
The Phil-IRI's individualized reading passages represent a significantly greater time constraint. In classrooms where up to 80% of learners may fall into the "Frustration" level or are classified as non-readers—a common reality in post-pandemic cohorts—the mathematical reality of one-on-one testing means that weeks of instructional time are sacrificed purely for diagnosis. For a class of 40 students where 30 fail the GST, the teacher must conduct 30 separate oral reading assessments, timing each reading, categorizing every miscue, and administering the comprehension tests. Teachers in rural, under-resourced districts, particularly those handling multi-grade classrooms, find this logistical demand entirely unsustainable. The excessive amount of time spent on administering assessments reduces the time teachers have available for actual classroom instruction, individualized student interventions, and engaging enrichment activities.

### **The Digitization Dilemma: Automated Scoresheets and Encoding Hurdles**

To consolidate national assessment data, DepEd relies on automated electronic scoresheets (e-CRLA and Phil-IRI automated forms) built on Microsoft Excel. These templates provide functionalities for encoding learner profiles, class record management, and school summaries. However, the digitization of these assessments has introduced a severe new layer of administrative pain, highlighting a digital divide in professional operations.  
Teachers are required to manually encode vast amounts of qualitative and quantitative data. For the Phil-IRI, this includes encoding the learner's starting grade level passage, the total number of words read, the number of miscues, reading time in minutes and seconds, and comprehension scores across literal, inferential, and critical questions. For the CRLA, teachers must encode scores for alphabet knowledge, phonological awareness, and word recognition across multiple languages into specific cells that generate automated reading profiles.  
The aggregation process is notoriously fragile and highly prone to human error. School ICT personnel or designated consolidating teachers must manually "Copy and Paste (as values)" data from individual classroom summaries into master school consolidation forms before uploading them to regional dashboards. A single formula error, a failure to paste "as values," or the use of an outdated version of the tool (a frequent occurrence given the rapid iteration of CRLA Version 2.0) can corrupt a school's entire database, leading to rejected submissions. For teachers lacking advanced computer literacy, or those in schools with limited technological facilities and poor internet connectivity, this encoding process requires taking administrative work home, leading to unpaid overtime, severe job dissatisfaction, and teacher burnout.

### **Competency Gaps and the Lack of Assessment Literacy**

The precision of diagnostic tools like the CRLA and Phil-IRI relies entirely on the clinical competency of the test administrator. Yet, systemic data reveals a critical gap in professional development. A recent empirical study evaluating the effectiveness of the CRLA found that despite 50% of surveyed teachers being highly experienced professionals (aged 46 and older with master’s coursework), a staggering 91.67% had participated in fewer than 20 hours of CRLA-related training.  
Furthermore, while the Phil-IRI has been in circulation longer, the granular skills required to accurately execute a Reading Miscue Inventory are highly specialized. Differentiating between a dialectical variation and a genuine decoding error, recognizing when a substitution maintains semantic meaning, or conducting a miscue analysis in real-time requires deep linguistic training that many generalist early-grade teachers have not received. Teachers repeatedly report feeling ill-equipped and unprepared to interpret the complex data generated by these tools, leading to discrepancies in how reading challenges are diagnosed across different regions. Without adequate "assessment literacy"—the ability to administer assessments in ways that support learning and effectively interpret the resulting data—teachers cannot translate raw scores into targeted, differentiated instructional plans.

## **The Financial and Material Realities of Reading Interventions**

Diagnostic assessments are fundamentally futile if they are not followed by robust, targeted interventions. In response to the alarming number of "Full Refresher" and "Frustration" level readers identified by the CRLA and Phil-IRI, DepEd mandated national intervention programs, most notably "Catch-Up Fridays"—a weekly learning initiative dedicating half the school day to reading activities and the other half to values, health, and peace education. However, the implementation of Catch-Up Fridays has exposed deep resource dependencies and shifted massive financial burdens directly onto the teachers.

### **Resource Dependency and Out-of-Pocket Expenditures**

According to Resource Dependency Theory, organizational effectiveness is intrinsically linked to the availability of external resources and instructional materials. Unfortunately, Catch-Up Fridays and localized remedial reading programs (such as Project Reach or Synergy) were implemented with severe deficits in state-provided resources. Teachers find themselves lacking context-based, grade-appropriate reading materials, decodable texts, and structured lesson plans tailored to the specific proficiency levels identified by the CRLA and Phil-IRI.  
Consequently, educators are forced to act as localized resource procurers. Teacher advocacy groups, such as the Teachers' Dignity Coalition (TDC) and the ACT Teachers party-list, note that it is highly unjust for teachers to shoulder the financial burden of a national program. Nevertheless, teachers frequently shell out personal funds, averaging approximately ₱300 monthly, to photocopy reading materials, activity sheets, and visual aids for their students. While some schools attempt to mitigate this by distributing soft copies, this assumes learners have access to digital devices and printers at home—a logistical impossibility for the majority of socio-economically disadvantaged populations in the public school system.

### **The Cost of Learner Engagement and Absenteeism**

Because Catch-Up Fridays are designed as non-graded enrichment and intervention days, student motivation to attend is notably low, leading to chronic absenteeism. Absenteeism undermines the validity of the entire reading program; students who desperately need reading support fall even further behind when they fail to attend the very interventions designed to help them.  
Teachers, desperate to ensure that the struggling readers identified by the CRLA actually receive the necessary intervention, resort to out-of-pocket incentivization. Educators purchase snacks, candies, and small prizes—often spending upwards of ₱100 weekly—to bribe students to attend class and maintain engagement during these remedial sessions. This dynamic highlights a profound systemic failure: the diagnostic tools effectively identify a crisis, macro-level policy dictates a solution, but the financial and material execution of that solution is subsidized entirely by the personal salaries of public school teachers.

### **Curricular Integration and Organizational Friction**

From the analytical lens of Organizational Theory and Cultural-Historical Activity Theory (CHAT), the execution of these reading programs reveals a massive contradiction between macro-policy goals and micro-level classroom realities. Catch-Up Fridays were implemented abruptly, causing immense role ambiguity and systemic inefficiency. Teachers—particularly novice educators with zero to three years of experience—report experiencing a "Symphony of Emotions," ranging from deep frustration to optimistic aspiration, driven by the intense stress of having to generate highly engaging, differentiated reading activities from scratch every single week without institutional support.  
The lack of structured, ready-made intervention packages aligned with Phil-IRI and CRLA diagnostic profiles forces teachers to spend their nights and weekends developing lesson plans that must simultaneously cater to "Low Emerging" readers who cannot recognize the alphabet, and "At Grade Level" readers who require advanced critical thinking tasks. This continuous cycle of planning, coupled with the pressure of regular academic subject instruction, accelerates teacher burnout, compromises their physical and mental well-being, and ultimately dilutes the effectiveness of the intervention. The Second Congressional Commission on Education (EDCOM 2\) corroborated these sentiments, noting that the program lacks the necessary structure, training, and differentiation to optimize learning recovery.

## **Pedagogical Nuances and Linguistic Complexities**

The Philippine archipelago is characterized by profound linguistic diversity, a reality that heavily impacts early literacy acquisition. The integration of Mother Tongue-Based Multilingual Education (MTB-MLE) into the CRLA represents both a major pedagogical advancement and a significant operational pain point for both teachers and students.

### **Mother Tongue Dialect Discrepancies**

Unlike the Phil-IRI, which strictly measures Filipino and English proficiency, the CRLA explicitly tests Key Stage 1 learners in their Mother Tongue, aligning with global research demonstrating that teaching children to read in their first language significantly accelerates literacy acquisition. However, the standardization of Mother Tongue assessments often clashes violently with hyper-local dialectical realities.  
Standardized CRLA tools translated into regional languages (e.g., Tagalog, Waray, Inabaknon) often employ formal vocabulary that differs substantially from the colloquial dialects spoken in the learners' homes. When a Grade 1 learner is assessed on phonemic awareness or vocabulary using a standardized formal Mother Tongue, they may fail to recognize the word—not because of a decoding deficit, but because of a vocabulary mismatch. Teachers struggle to navigate this discrepancy, as strictly adhering to the standardized tool may yield artificially low scores, while translating or contextualizing the assessment into the local vernacular compromises the tool's standardized validity and reliability. Furthermore, for learners whose primary language at home is English, taking a Mother Tongue assessment inevitably leads to failure, prompting urgent administrative calls for the release of specialized English-as-L1 CRLA packages.

### **The Trauma of the Multilingual Transition**

The Philippine curriculum expects learners to transition seamlessly from their Mother Tongue in Grade 1, to Filipino in Grade 2, and English in Grade 3\. The CRLA tracks this progression by administering multi-language toolkits in Grades 2 and 3\. This rapid linguistic accumulation creates immense cognitive load for early readers. A Grade 3 learner who is still categorized as a "Moderate Refresher" in their Mother Tongue is simultaneously expected to decode complex consonant blends (e.g., *l*, *r*, *s* blends), vowel digraphs (e.g., *ai*, *ea*, *ee*), and diphthongs (e.g., *oy*, *ou*, *ow*) in English.  
When the Phil-IRI Group Screening Test in Filipino is introduced in Grade 3, the linguistic cognitive overload peaks. Students who have not yet secured automaticity in word recognition in their native language are suddenly tested on silent reading comprehension in a second or third language. The Vygotskian concept of the Zone of Proximal Development (ZPD) is frequently violated here; the assessments present tasks so far beyond the learners' current scaffolded capabilities that they induce immediate psychological withdrawal and anxiety.

## **The Learner's Experience: Cognitive and Emotional Pain Points**

While the administrative burdens on teachers are thoroughly documented, the psychological and emotional toll on the Grade 1 to 3 learners taking the CRLA and Phil-IRI is equally critical. For many children, these diagnostic assessments are their first formal academic evaluations, taking place in the fragile developmental window following years of pandemic-induced isolation and learning loss.

### **Assessment Anxiety and Behavioral Resistance**

The individual, one-on-one nature of the CRLA and the Phil-IRI Graded Passages is inherently intimidating for early-grade learners. Unlike a written group quiz where a student can anonymously guess an answer, reading aloud to an evaluator is a highly exposed, vulnerable act. If assessments are perceived as stressful, irrelevant, or overly difficult, Grade 2 learners may actively disengage, leading to results that do not accurately reflect their true capabilities.  
Teachers report significant behavioral and emotional resistance from students during the administration of the CRLA. In numerous instances, when students realized they were required to read individually, they asked for permission to leave the room and did not return. Others cried uncontrollably when their names were called, while some exhibited trauma-like "freeze" responses—staring blankly at the assessment tool and refusing to open their mouths. Learners who lack decoding automaticity often resort to spelling out words letter-by-letter, painfully dragging out the assessment and experiencing visible frustration.  
The Phil-IRI explicitly categorizes the lowest tier of reading proficiency as the "Frustration" level—a clinical term that aptly describes a state where the learner refuses to read, showing active withdrawal from reading situations due to the overwhelming difficulty of the text. The psychological scarring caused by repeated failure during these assessments can lead to a persistent disengagement from the educational process, destroying the learner's self-confidence and intrinsic motivation to read.

### **The Depth of Post-Pandemic Learning Poverty**

The data generated by these assessments illuminates a bleak reality regarding the depth of learning poverty. Across various districts, the beginning-of-school-year (BoSY) CRLA results frequently show that up to 97% of incoming early-grade classes are not grade-ready, requiring intensive support in alphabet knowledge and phonemic awareness. In one longitudinal study involving Grade 1 pupils, out of 213 assessed learners at the beginning of the year, 142 (66%) were classified as "Full Refreshers," completely unable to decode basic Mother Tongue words.  
Similarly, Phil-IRI data historically reveals massive clusters of students trapped at the Frustration level. A study evaluating Grade 8 students (highlighting the compounding nature of unchecked early reading deficits) found that a staggering 98.7% of respondents fell under the "Frustration" level. The inability to read is not an isolated academic deficit; because literacy is the gateway to all other subjects, these learners experience compounding failures across the curriculum.

### **Socio-Economic and Familial Barriers**

The struggles of early-grade learners are inextricably tied to their socio-economic environments. The success of early reading interventions relies heavily on home-school collaboration and parental reinforcement. However, educators note a severe lack of parental involvement. Many parents, particularly in rural or economically disadvantaged communities, lack the time, resources, or their own literacy skills necessary to assist their children with reading practice at home.  
During the prolonged pandemic school closures, many children had no physical contact with the school system, and parents were unable to guide their early literacy development effectively. Socioeconomic disparities significantly impact reading outcomes, with rural and economically disadvantaged communities showing reading proficiency rates up to 40% lower than their urban counterparts. Consequently, the entire burden of moving a child from the "Full Refresher" or "Frustration" level to "Independent" proficiency rests solely on the shoulders of the classroom teacher during the limited hours of the school week.

## **Intervention Efficacy and Longitudinal Outcomes**

Despite the immense pain points associated with the administration of the CRLA and Phil-IRI, the empirical data suggests that when the assessments are completed and interventions are subsequently applied, the tools are highly effective in driving literacy recovery.  
Longitudinal analyses demonstrate significant improvements in reading proficiency profiles across the academic year. Data from the Department of Education indicates that targeted interventions driven by CRLA and Phil-IRI results helped 4.5 million struggling readers improve their literacy skills by the end of School Year 2025-2026. The number of learners requiring urgent intervention (Full Refreshers) dropped drastically from 6.7 million at the start of the school year to 2.2 million. The highest decrease in struggling readers occurred in Grades 1 to 3, which recorded a massive 33 percentage point drop, while "grade-level ready" readers surged from 3.3 million to 5.8 million.  
Granular school-level studies corroborate these national trends. In the Schools Division of Makati, longitudinal tracking of the CRLA from 2022 to 2025 revealed dramatic reductions in students requiring full refresher interventions: Grade 1 dropped from 55.17% to 1.64%, and Grade 2 dropped from 32.17% to 0.80%. At Ani-e Elementary School in Negros Occidental, an early intervention program for Grade 4-6 non-readers driven by Phil-IRI data saw learners demonstrate significant progress, moving to the "Instructional" level in word reading (M=91.70) and reading comprehension (M=62.23). Furthermore, a study in Eastern Samar showed that prior to a locally-funded remedial program based on Phil-IRI diagnostics, 82% of learners were categorized as struggling or frustrated; post-assessment, 70% had progressed to instructional or independent reading levels.

| Assessment Metric | Pre-Intervention (BoSY) Status | Post-Intervention (EoSY) Status | Improvement Metric |
| :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- |
| **National Urgent Intervention (DepEd Data)** | 6.7 Million Learners | 2.2 Million Learners | 67% Reduction in Frustration/Full Refresher levels. |
| **Grade Ready Readers (DepEd Data)** | 3.3 Million Learners | 5.8 Million Learners | 75% Increase in Independent readers. |
| **Makati City CRLA (Grade 1\)** | 55.17% Full Refresher | 1.64% Full Refresher | Near-total eradication of lowest reading tier. |
| **Ani-e Elementary (Word Reading)** | Non-reader baseline | M \= 91.70 (Instructional) | Highly significant progress in decoding skills. |

These results suggest that systematic early intervention, particularly when combined with Mother Tongue-based instruction, creates a robust foundation for sustained literacy development. The diagnostic tools, despite their administrative flaws, successfully provide the baseline data required to execute these life-altering interventions.

## **Second-Order Implications and The Intervention-Instruction Paradox**

The intersection of diagnostic mandates, resource scarcity, and learning poverty generates cascading effects that impact the broader educational ecosystem. The most profound third-order effect of the current assessment regime is the "Intervention-Instruction Paradox."  
Diagnostic tools like the CRLA and Phil-IRI are designed to buy time—to quickly identify exactly what a child needs so that instruction can be hyper-targeted and efficient. However, because the tools are logistically cumbersome to administer and encode, and because the resulting interventions (like Catch-Up Fridays) lack structured, ready-made resources, the process of *diagnosing* the problem actively consumes the time and energy required to *solve* the problem. The diagnostic process cannibalizes instructional capacity. A teacher who spends 40 hours a month administering one-on-one reading assessments and encoding buggy Excel dashboards has 40 fewer hours to spend on explicit phonics instruction, guided reading, and curriculum contextualization.  
Furthermore, the immense pressure placed on teachers to submit consolidated data to regional dashboards creates a substantial risk to data integrity. When educators are fatigued, under-resourced, and overwhelmed by the encoding demands of the automated templates, the likelihood of data manipulation—whether conscious or unconscious—increases. Teachers may inadvertently speed through assessments, fail to accurately record complex miscues, or "teach to the test" to demonstrate artificial progress between the Beginning of School Year (BoSY) and End of School Year (EoSY) assessments. If the data fed into the national literacy dashboards is flawed by administrative fatigue, the subsequent macro-level policy decisions and budget allocations made by the Department of Education will be fundamentally misaligned with the true needs of the learners.

## **Policy Shifts and Future Outlook: Mitigating Assessment Fatigue**

Recognizing the unsustainable nature of assessment overload, the Department of Education has initiated systemic reforms. The issuance of DepEd Order No. 15, s. 2026, marks a pivotal shift in educational evaluation policy. By phasing out the grade transmutation policy (where a raw score of 60 was artificially converted to a passing 75), the DepEd aims to strengthen accountability and provide teachers and parents with a transparent, uninflated view of a learner's genuine capability.  
Crucially, the 2026 guidelines seek to drastically reduce assessment fatigue by setting recommended caps on written works, performance tasks, and term exams, thereby easing the burden on educators and allowing them to focus on formative feedback rather than endless quantitative grading. By shifting Kindergarten to Grade 3 toward a descriptive, non-numerical grading system over the coming years, the DepEd is acknowledging the developmental fragility of early learners, aiming to reduce the psychological trauma associated with constant numerical evaluation. Furthermore, the modernization of these policies, including establishing the first rules on Artificial Intelligence (AI) in assessment (strictly governing AI-assisted outputs and data privacy), indicates a forward-looking approach to streamlining the workload of the Philippine teacher.

## **Synthesis and Strategic Pathways**

The administration of the CRLA and Phil-IRI for Key Stage 1 learners represents a critical, yet highly strained, mechanism within the Philippine basic education system. While the intent—eradicating learning poverty through data-driven, targeted reading intervention—is undeniably vital and proven effective by longitudinal data, the ground-level execution is hampered by systemic dysfunctions that place untenable operational, financial, and psychological burdens on both the teacher and the child.  
The path forward requires a holistic recalibration of the literacy assessment ecosystem. The Department of Education must address the assessment convergence in Grade 3, streamlining the diagnostic pathway to merge the rapid triage of the CRLA with the granular insights of the Phil-IRI, thereby eliminating redundant testing. Furthermore, if initiatives like "Catch-Up Fridays" are to succeed, the state must centralize the development and distribution of highly structured, context-based, decodable reading materials that directly align with CRLA and Phil-IRI proficiency tiers. The financial and material burden of national interventions must be completely removed from the personal purview of the public school teacher.  
Technological optimization is equally paramount; the architecture of automated scoresheets must be refined into seamless, cloud-based assessment applications that eliminate manual copy-pasting and redundant data entry, drastically reducing the administrative hours stolen from instructional time. Finally, massive investments in "assessment literacy" training are required, equipping teachers with the clinical skills to interpret miscue data, design differentiated lesson plans, and create supportive, low-anxiety testing environments for children. Ultimately, the transition from diagnostic awareness to actual reading proficiency cannot be achieved through assessment alone; it requires a deeply resourced, empathetic educational infrastructure that supports the cognitive development of the early-grade learner while fiercely protecting the pedagogical capacity of the teacher.
