A Grade 1 teacher has forty children and a legal duty to find out which of them cannot read.

She does it one child at a time, out loud, stopwatch in hand, while the other thirty-nine wait. Eight to fifteen minutes each. Three times a year. In Grade 3, twice over: once in English, once in Filipino.

That is about forty hours a year. A working week spent finding out, not helping.

Then every score is typed into a government spreadsheet and uploaded before a deadline. So the week gets rationed. Rounds get shortened. And the child who reads slowest takes longest, so he is the first one skipped.

02

The government's form, filled in.

Nothing retyped. ReadiLY uses DepEd's own formula for words correct per minute and accuracy, and exports the spreadsheet the national portal accepts.

Works with no internet.

Recording, scoring, the class dashboard and the export all work in airplane mode. When a connection appears, only scores sync. Never audio.

04

Every flagged word is a tappable item. Change any judgement and the score recalculates. The record saves as teacher-adjusted.

05

Made for Filipino classrooms.

Common accent substitutions, such as p for f, b for v and t for th, count as correct by default, as DepEd's rules require. Passages are original and set where children live: the sari-sari store, the jeepney, the fiesta.

Why it is stricter

A reading test should not flatter a struggling reader.

Most modern speech software guesses what you meant and tidies up the transcript. That helps with dictation. For a reading test it is the wrong thing to do: a child who stumbles, skips and misreads can come out sounding fine. The score rises for exactly the children the test exists to find.

ReadiLY uses a stricter kind of speech recogniser. It writes down only what it hears and cannot invent a word the child did not say.

These are build targets, not results. Two trained human examiners scoring the same child disagree about one time in five. Matching a careful human is our bar.

No child's voice leaves the tablet.

Under Philippine law, a child's voice is biometric personal information and their score is sensitive personal information. We built the app around that.

Audio stays on the device.

Not for processing, not for backup.

The microphone stays off until consent is confirmed.

The teacher must confirm a signed parental consent form is on file. Families who decline get a non-digital alternative.

The school owns the data.

We are only the processor, under a written agreement.

Scores sync without names.

The cloud sees initials and the learner reference number, never a full name. A school can use the app with no account at all.
